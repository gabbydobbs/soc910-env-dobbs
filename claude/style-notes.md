# Gabrielle Dobbs — academic writing style notes

Compiled from: SOC 824 article review (Stivers & Timmermans critique), SOC 824
literature review (physician burnout/radiology), cultural sociology abstract
(thrifting culture) — plus structure-only observations (no quoted content)
from the SOC 780 final paper and the Capitalism seminar paper, both of which
draw on protected thesis interview data (see note at the bottom).

## Structure

- Opens with stakes/scope before argument: states the broad problem or
  existing consensus, pivots to what's underexamined, then states her own
  argument explicitly ("This paper argues...", "This work will attempt to...").
- Named theoretical frameworks act as organizing spines, not just citations —
  walked through (definitions, mechanisms) before being applied to the case.
- Formal article reviews follow a fixed labeled-section formula: Overview →
  Data and Methods → Results and Conclusion → Implications → Critiques, each
  a dense paragraph, not bullets.
- Literature reviews use topic-based subheadings (not chronological),
  synthesizing multiple sources per paragraph rather than one-source-per-
  paragraph summaries.
- Conclusions restate the throughline plus explicit forward-looking
  implications ("Addressing X requires...", "Future research should...").

## Sentence and paragraph craft

- Long, clause-dense sentences that stack qualifiers and citations
  mid-sentence, not only at the end.
- Topic sentences do real work — they name the claim, not just the topic.
- Comfortable shifting register within a sentence: jargon immediately
  followed by a plain-language gloss ("environmental complexity refers
  to...").
- States claims directly, then supports them — doesn't hedge before stating.

## Tone and voice

- Third person, minimal first-person, even on more personal topics (the
  thrifting piece says "this work," not "I").
- Register shifts by subject: formal-clinical in health/medical sociology
  pieces; more playful and culturally fluent when the topic is internet/
  consumer culture (comfortable putting slang in scare quotes: "thrift
  gods," "vintage").

## Citation style

- APA in-text citations, often with page numbers even for paraphrase.
- Chains 2-4 citations together at key claims rather than one per sentence.

## Formatting conventions

- Course papers open with a title block: title, name, department/course/
  instructor/date — not just title + name.
- Section headers are descriptive phrases specific to the argument
  ("The Autonomy Paradox and Status-Based Distress"), never generic labels.
- Full APA reference list, alphabetized, DOIs included where available.

## Slide/visual aesthetic

Compiled from two Google Slides decks (connector access fixed 2026-09-28):
"Burnout and the Human Cost of Productivity in Radiology" (M.A. thesis
defense-style deck) and "HP&M 901 Final Presentation" (policy analysis). A
third candidate ("Academic Article Presentation: Budget Neutrality...")
wasn't sampled yet — check it too if these patterns ever look inconsistent.

**Color system** — the same design tokens appear in both a dark and a light
variant, not two unrelated palettes:
- Dark variant: background `#0f172a` (deep navy/slate), text in light
  slate/white (`#e6e7f0`, `#cbd4e1`, `#f7fafc`), accent `#37bdf7` (sky blue),
  white (`#ffffff`) used for card/box fills.
- Light variant: background `#cee1f3` (pale blue), text in dark slate
  (`#334155`, `#0f172a`, `#1e293b`), accent `#3b82f6` (blue), white and
  `#e1e7f0` for card fills.
- These read as a Tailwind-CSS-style slate+blue palette (the hex values
  match Tailwind's default `slate-900`/`sky-400`/`blue-500` tokens closely),
  suggesting a consistent template/generation approach rather than
  per-slide manual color picking — reproduce the tokens, not necessarily
  the exact tool.

**Typography** — consistent font trio across both decks: **Lato** as the
main workhorse body font (highest usage by far), **Merriweather** (serif)
for headers/emphasis, **DM Sans** used secondarily. Occasional Arial/Calibri
appearances are pasted-content leftovers, not intentional choices — don't
treat those as part of the palette.

**Title slide convention**: title stated as a claim/finding rather than a
topic label (e.g. "From Volume to Value: Policy Levers for Stabilizing the
Radiology Workforce," "Burnout and the Human Cost of Productivity in
Radiology"), a descriptive subtitle line, then a byline. For coursework:
`COURSE NUMBER—Name`. For thesis-level work: name + degree candidacy +
university/year, then a committee line.

**Content slide layout**: information organized into short card-like blocks
— a bolded micro-header (a few words, e.g. "Prevalence," "An Occupational
Syndrome") followed by one or two supporting sentences with an inline
citation — multiple such blocks per slide, rather than one long bullet list.

## Data-sensitivity note — read before reusing this file

Two of the five source papers reviewed to build this file contain extensive
verbatim quotes from the user's protected thesis interview data (the same
26-radiologist study behind the excluded `Thesis/` folder). No quotes,
participant descriptions, or pseudonyms from that data were carried into
this summary — only generic structural/craft patterns.

**Going forward:** any file, regardless of which folder or course it's
filed under, containing quotes attributed to pseudonymous "Dr. [Name]"
radiologists describing workplace stress/burnout is drawn from that same
protected dataset. Treat it like the `Thesis/` folder — don't reproduce
quotes from it or use it as a style source without asking first.
